# Research: Workplace Accommodations for Oregon State Employees

Basis for every question, label, and generated line in the Oregon Accommodation
Request Builder. All claims below carry inline citations to the ledger
(`.hermes/citations/ledger.json`); the Sources block is generated mechanically
from that ledger.

Scope: employees of **Oregon state government** (all state agencies, including
temporary employees). The DAS Chief Human Resources Office issues Statewide
Policies that each agency administers.[1] Unofficial notes are marked as such.

## 1. The four accommodation tracks

Oregon state employees have four distinct accommodation channels. Each has its
own trigger, statute, and typical requests — so the tool asks a different
question set for each.

**Disability.** An employer may not refuse to accommodate the "known physical
or mental limitations of a qualified individual with a disability who is a job
applicant or employee, unless the employer can demonstrate that the
accommodation would impose an undue hardship."[3] Statewide Policy 50.020.10
operationalizes this for state service: it applies to "all state employees,
including state temporary employees," each agency names an ADA coordinator, and
the agency "must engage in an interactive dialogue with the employee to
determine whether the accommodation is necessary and will be effective."[1]

**Pregnancy, childbirth, or a related medical condition.** ORS 659A.147 makes
it an unlawful employment practice to "[f]ail or refuse to make reasonable
accommodation to the known limitations" related to "pregnancy, childbirth or a
related medical condition, including but not limited to lactation."[6] The
statute also prohibits requiring an employee to take family leave "if the
employer can make reasonable accommodation to the known limitations."[6] ORS
659A.146 lists representative accommodations: "acquisition or modification of
equipment or devices," "more frequent or longer break periods or periodic
rest," "assistance with manual labor," and "modification of work schedules or
job assignments."[5] Statewide Policy 50.020.10 explicitly folds these
pregnancy accommodations into the definition of reasonable accommodation.[1]

**Expressing milk at work.** ORS 653.077 requires employers to "provide
reasonable unpaid rest periods to accommodate an employee who needs to express
milk," each time the employee has a need, and to make "reasonable efforts to
provide a location, other than a public restroom or toilet stall, in close
proximity to the employee's work area" where the employee can express milk in
private.[7] Employees "shall provide reasonable notice to the employer that the
employee intends to express milk upon returning to work after the child's
birth" when possible — and "failure to give notice … is not grounds for
discipline."[7] For state employees, Statewide Policy 60.000.30 goes further
than the statute: it provides rest periods "for up to two years after the birth
of the child" (the statute sets the floor at a child of 18 months), treats the
rest periods as paid to the extent paid rest periods are required, allows
make-up time, and counts unpaid expression breaks as hours worked for health
insurance purposes.[2] BOLI's worker-facing pages confirm the 18-month statutory
protection and the private-location rule.[9][10]

**Religious observance.** ORS 659A.033 (the operative part of the Oregon
Workplace Religious Freedom Act) is violated when an employer does not allow
use of vacation or other unrestricted leave for religious observance, or
"imposes an occupational requirement that restricts the ability of an employee
to wear religious clothing in accordance with the employee's sincerely held
religious beliefs, to take time off for a holy day or to take time off to
participate in a religious observance or practice," where reasonable
accommodation would not impose undue hardship and the activities have "only a
temporary or tangential impact" on essential functions.[4]

## 2. The interactive process — and its deadlines

OAR 839-006-0206 makes the process mandatory: "Once a qualified employee …
has requested reasonable accommodation **or otherwise disclosed** … a
disability that may require reasonable accommodation, the employer has a duty
to initiate a meaningful interactive process," and failure to do so "is a
failure to reasonably accommodate in violation of ORS 659A.112(2)(e)."[8] The
rule defines a meaningful interactive process as "an informal process … in an
effort to identify potential reasonable accommodation."[8]

Statewide Policy 50.020.10 sets the state-service timeline: "Agencies will
acknowledge in writing all written requests for accommodations within seven
calendar days from the date of receipt."[1] It also requires that the agency
"must give primary consideration to the specific accommodation requested by the
employee" while reserving the ability to "identify and provide an alternative
accommodation" through the interactive process, notes the duty is "ongoing"
(both sides re-engage "if an accommodation proves ineffective"), and permits
denial only if the accommodation is not effective, causes undue hardship, or
creates imminent physical risk — "only after careful consideration" of
alternatives.[1]

A sample agency procedure (Oregon Military Department) shows the typical
flow after submission: supervisor supplies the essential functions of the
position; the ADA coordinator prepares a letter to the health care provider; an
interactive meeting is held with the employee, supervisor, and ADA coordinator;
a determination is made and communicated by letter.[14]

## 3. What the official request form asks

The DAS sample Reasonable Accommodation Request form collects: name, employee
number, classification title, section/work unit, work location, work phone,
supervisor name; then four open questions — the condition impacting the
employee's ability to perform essential functions; how long the employee has
experienced it and how long they expect it to continue; "please explain
specifically what you cannot do or need to do differently"; and "please
describe the accommodation you are requesting and how it will enable you to
perform the essential functions of your job," plus "any other information that
would help us evaluate your request."[13]

The same form notes two things this tool preserves: completing a form is
"helpful, but employees are not required to complete it in order to request an
accommodation" (verbal requests are equally valid), and "medical information
may be necessary as part of the interactive process."[13]

The tool's questions map 1:1 onto that form — identity block, limitation,
duration, requested accommodation and how it helps, other information — then
adds track-specific detail (child's age for lactation, conflict description
for religious) that the agencies' interactive processes surface anyway.

## 4. What "reasonable accommodation" means (and does not)

Per Statewide Policy 50.020.10, reasonable accommodation is a "change or
adjustment to a job or work environment that enables a qualified employee with
a disability to perform the essential functions of a job, or to enjoy the
benefits and privileges of employment equal to those enjoyed by employees
without disabilities." It does **not** include "lowering production standards,
promoting or assigning an employee to a higher-paying job, creating a
position, or reassigning essential functions to another worker," nor personal
aids used on and off the job.[1] OAR 839-006-0206 lists examples: accessible
facilities; "job restructuring, such as part-time or modified work schedules
or reassignment to vacant positions"; acquiring or modifying equipment;
adjusting examinations, training materials or policies; qualified readers or
interpreters; and a leave of absence.[8]

## 5. Documentation and confidentiality

For disability requests, functional medical documentation may be requested
from the employee's provider as part of the interactive process.[1][13] BOLI
maintains an employer-facing accommodation toolkit with sample provider
questionnaires, approval and denial letters, and reassignment guidelines that
agencies use alongside the statewide policy.[11] The
OMD sample procedure states that "All requests for reasonable accommodations
and any related medical information will be kept separate from the
applicant/employee's personnel file" and kept confidential "except to the
extent a supervisor must be notified to implement an approved
accommodation."[14] Retaliation is prohibited both by statewide policy[1] and
by statute for pregnancy-related requests ("[t]ake an adverse employment
action or in any manner discriminate or retaliate against an applicant or an
employee … because the applicant or employee has inquired about, requested or
used a reasonable accommodation").[6] BOLI's worker guidance states the same
for disability and religion requests.[12]

The tool deliberately does **not** ask for a diagnosis. The statutory triggers
are "known limitations"[3][6] and, for the interactive process, any request or
disclosure that an accommodation "may require."[8] An employee can describe
functional limitations ("what I cannot do or need to do differently," in the
official form's words[13]) and the documentation question offers the four
honest postures — can obtain, already have, have questions, prefer to talk
first.

## 6. Notes on the tool's structure

- Two outputs: the employee's request text (to paste into an email/document
  for the manager) and a manager-facing summary containing exactly what HR /
  Employee Services needs, with fill-in brackets for manager-only fields
  (date received, acknowledgment due date). This is the timesaver the tool
  exists for: no check-back for missing information.
- No storage of any kind: the generator is pure string-building over
  in-memory answers.[unverified as a legal matter — by design: there is no
  backend, no localStorage, no network call in the code.]
- Law citations appear in a footer line (toggleable) rather than as demands:
  the goal is a complete, routable request, not an adversarial letter.
